                                                                                 1. Introduction
                                              Roe algebras are C∗ -algebras associated to metric spaces, which encode their
                                           coarse geometric information. They were introduced by John Roe in [22], where
                                           he showed that their K-theories can serve as receptacles for higher indices of
                                           elliptic differential operators on open manifolds. Hence, the computation of the
                                           K-theories of Roe algebras becomes crucial in the study of higher index theory, and
                                           has lead to significant progress in topology, geometry, and analysis (e.g., [23, 24]).
                                           There is also a uniform version of the Roe algebra, which equally plays a key role
                                           in higher index theory (see [28]).
                                              Although the Roe algebra is important, it is usually hard to tell whether a given
                                           operator belongs to it or not. To overcome the issue, John Roe introduced the
                                           notion of quasi-local operator in [22]. The set of all quasi-local operators forms
                                           a C∗ -algebra, called the quasi-local algebra, which is formally larger than the Roe
                                           algebra. As shown by Engel in [5], the higher indices of elliptic pseudo-differential
                                           operators on open manifolds naturally live in the K-theories of the quasi-local
                                           algebra. Hence, the following question is crucial and has drawn considerable
                                           attention over the past few years:
                                           Question. When does the Roe algebra coincide with the quasi-local algebra? Do
                                           their K-theories coincide as well?
                                             To be more precise, let us explain the notions for the uniform case. Let (X, d)

Main Theorem I. Let {(Xn , dFn )}n∈N be a sequence of finite metric spaces of uniformly
bounded geometry, and X := n∈N Xn be their coarse disjoint union. Let M ∈ N, and
H0 be a Hilbert space. For each n ∈ N, let Wn ∈ B(ℓ2 (Xn ; H0 )) be aPpartial isometry

   When M = 1, Main Theorem I recovers the main results of [10, 16]. Although
the formulation is similar to the block-rank-one case, the proof is totally different
and divided into three technique parts. Let us explain the idea in more details.
   Step I. As in the block-rank-one case, firstly we would like to compute the
norm χA PχB for a discrete metric space X, a rank M projection P ∈ B(ℓ2 (X)) and
A, B ⊆ X. This is quite easy when M = 1 (see [16, Lemma 4.3]), however, more
C∗ -algebraic techniques are required in the general case. It turns out that
                 ∥χA PU χB ∥ = r(m(A) · m(B))1/2 = m(A)1/2 m(B)1/2 .
Here m(A) is the Gram matrix for the restrictions of an orthonormal basis of
Ran(P), which forms a matrix-valued probability measure on X, and r(·) denotes
the spectral radius of a matrix. See Proposition 3.2 for details. Unfortunately based
on this computation, we do not know how to find an appropriate graphic object
(as the measured asymptotic expanders in the block-rank-one case) to characterise
the quasi-locality of P. Hence we need a totally new approach to proceed.
  Step II. This is the most technical part of the proof. A natural idea is trying
to decompose a rank-M quasi-local projection into a sum of M-many rank-one
quasi-local projections. If this can be done, then the proof follows directly from
the rank-one case. Although it is unclear whether the actual decomposition can
always be achieved, we manage to find an approximating version in a quantitative
way. Roughly speaking, we show the following (see Theorem 3.1 for a precise
and refined statement we need). Note that we do not even need the condition of
bounded geometry on the space.
Theorem A. Let (X, d) be a discrete metric space, and P ∈ B(ℓ2 (X)) a rank-M projection.
Assume P is (ε, R)-quasi-local for some ε, R > 0, i.e., for any A, B ⊆ X with d(A, B) > R
we have ∥χA PχB ∥ < ε. Then there exists ε′ , R′ > 0 only depending on M, ε, R with
ε′ → 0 as ε → 0, and an orthonormal basis {u(1) , · · · , u(M) } of Ran(P) such that each
rank-one projection onto Cu(i) is (ε′ , R′ )-quasi-local for each i.
  The idea of the proof for Theorem A can be summarised as follows: We start by
choosing an arbitrary orthonormal basis of Ran(P). If one of the associated rank-
one projections are not quasi-local in the allowed error, then apply a correction
technique (Proposition 3.6) which is based on Step I, to modify the originally
chosen basis to a new one and show that they have nicer quasi-local behaviours.
  Step III. It remains to deal with (ε, R)-quasi-local rank-one projections, and
we show that they can be approximated by finite propagation operators within
controlled error. This is indeed a quantitative version for the rank-one result
established in [10, 16] by chasing parameters (see also the recent work [17]). We
obtain the following (see Theorem 4.1 and Theorem 4.2 for precise statements):
Theorem B. Let (X, d) be a discrete metric space, and P ∈ B(ℓ2 (X)) a rank-one partial
isometry which is (ε, R)-quasi-local for some ε, R > 0. Then there exists ε′ , R′ > 0 only
depending on ε, R and the bounded geometry information of X with ε′ → 0 as ε → 0,
and T ∈ B(ℓ2 (X)) with propagation at most R such that ∥P − T∥ ≤ ε′ .
  Combining the three steps above, we conclude the proof of Main Theorem I.
