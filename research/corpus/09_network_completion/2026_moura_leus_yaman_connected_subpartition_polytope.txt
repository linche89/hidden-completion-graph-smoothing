                                                                                              Abstract
                                                         Let k be a positive integer and let G be a graph with n vertices. A connected k-
                                                     subpartition of G is a collection of k pairwise disjoint sets (a.k.a. classes) of vertices
                                                     in G such that each set induces a connected subgraph. The connected k-subpartition
                                                     polytope of G, denoted by P(G, k), is defined as the convex hull of the incidence vectors
                                                     of all connected k-subpartitions of G. Many applications arising in off-shore oil-drilling,
                                                     forest planning, image processing, cluster analysis, political districting, police patrolling,
                                                     and biology are modeled in terms of finding connected (sub)partitions of a graph. This
                                                     study focuses on the facial structure of P(G, k) and the computational complexity of the
                                                     corresponding separation problems. We first propose a set of valid inequalities having
                                                     non-zero coefficients associated with a single class that extends and generalizes the ones
                                                     in the literature of related problems, show sufficient conditions for these inequalities to
                                                     be facet-defining, and design a polynomial-time separation algorithm for them. We also
                                                     devise two sets of inequalities that consider multiple classes, prove when they define
                                                     facets, and study the computational complexity of associated separation problems. Fi-
                                                     nally, we report on computational experiments showing the usefulness of the proposed
                                                     inequalities.

                                              Keywords: Connectivity problems, Polyhedral study, Facets, Separation algorithms,
                                           Branch and cut

     Many combinatorial problems deal with finding (sub)partitions of the set of vertices of
a graph into classes that induce connected subgraphs, or equivalently integer points in the
polytope P. A well-studied example is the Maximum Weight Connected Subgraph
problem, which receives as input a vertex-weighted graph G (weights may be negative), and
consists in finding a connected subgraph of G that maximizes the sum of the weights of its
vertices (see Álvarez-Miranda et al. [4], and Hochbaum and Pathria [18]).
     Convex Recoloring is another problem involving partitioning graphs into connected
subgraphs that has received considerable attention in the last years (see Campêlo et al. [9],
and Chopra et al. [12]). This problem was introduced by Moran and Snir [25], and consists
in finding a (re)coloring of a given colored graph such that each color class in the recoloring
induces a connected subgraph, and the number of recolored vertices is minimized. Note
that the concept of coloring here differs from the classical definition of (proper) coloring,
where pairs of vertices linked by an edge are assigned distinct colors.
     In the Balanced Connected Partition problem, the goal is to partition a graph
into a given number of connected subgraphs so that the size of every subgraph is roughly
the same. This concept of balance can be modeled, for example, as minimizing (resp.
maximizing) the size of the largest (resp. smallest) subgraph in the partition, or minimizing
the maximum difference of sizes between two subgraphs. These objectives yield equivalent
problems when one has to partition into two classes, but lead to distinct problems when the
desired partition has more than two subgraphs (see Lucertini et al. [22]). A related problem
investigated by Lovász [21] and Györi [17] consists in partitioning a connected graph into a
given number of connected subgraphs with prescribed orders.
     Another example of connected partition problem is Connected Maximum k-Cut, a
variant of the classical Maximum k-Cut in which the goal is to find a partition of the
vertices into k classes inducing connected subgraphs such that the number of edges linking
vertices in distinct classes is maximized. Recently, Hojny et al. [19] proposed mixed-integer
linear programming formulations for an edge-weighted version of this problem, and reported
on extensive computational experiments.
     The idea of dividing graphs into connected parts (classes) plays an essential role in
many applications arising in off-shore oil-drilling, forest planning, image processing, cluster
analysis, political districting, police patrolling, and biology. For more details on these
applications, we refer to [5, 10, 18, 22, 23, 24, 25].

    The facial structure of the polytope P(G, k) was studied before by Campêlo et al. [8] in
the context of a polyhedral approach to Convex Recoloring. Chopra et al. [12] provided
a strong extended formulation for the problem on trees, and reported on computational
experiments. We remark that Convex Recoloring is NP-hard even on paths, as shown
by Moran and Snir [25]. Indeed, Moura and Wakabayashi [26] proved that this problem on
n-vertex bipartite graphs cannot be approximated within a factor of n1−ε for any ε > 0,
unless P = NP.

so-called connected subgraph polytope. More precisely, assuming that G is connected, they

shown by Wang et al. [29] also assumes that G is connected. Hence, even for the basic
inequalities in the formulation, their facetness may depend on the graph being connected.
For the sake of simplicity in our proofs, we henceforth assume that G is a connected graph.

Contributions
In Section 2 we introduce a class of inequalities for P(G, k) that generalizes both the con-
nectivity inequalities (2) and the indegree inequalities (4). The proposed class contains
all nontrivial inequalities studied by Wang et al. [29] for the connected subgraph polytope
(i.e., k = 1), and by Chopra et al. [12] and Campêlo et al. [7] for P(G, k) with k ≥ 1.
Furthermore, it contains new strong valid inequalities. We also prove sufficient (weak) con-
ditions under which the proposed generalized inequalities are facet-defining for P(G, k). If
the input graph G is a tree, we show that all facets of P(G, k) having non-zero coefficients
associated with a single class in [k] are induced by indegree inequalities (4).
     The separation problem associated with these inequalities is investigated in Section 3.
More precisely, we design a polynomial-time algorithm that reduces the input instance
to a quadratic number of bipartite instances of the Minimum Weight Vertex Cover
problem, when the partition defining the inequality is fixed. In general, this algorithm
runs in O(k|V (G)||E(G)|) time. When separating inequalities (2) and (4), this algorithm
achieves the same running times as the corresponding separation routines for these inequal-
ities described in the literature. The proposed routine runs in O(k(|V (G)| + |E(G)|)) time
when separating a set of inequalities that strictly contains the indegree inequalities (4).
     In Section 4, we propose two sets of valid inequalities, namely multiway and pairing
inequalities, that combine multiple classes. We characterize the facets induced by multiway
inequalities, and present sufficient conditions for the pairing inequalities to be facet-defining.
We show in Section 5 that the separation problem associated with multiway inequalities is
NP-hard (if the stable set which defines it is given) as this separation boils down to solving

                                                4
the Multiway Cut problem. Moreover, we show that the separation problem of the pairing
inequalities can solved in polynomial time if the graph is a tree. The proposed algorithm
computes a minimum-cost flow in a network which is constructed with the vertices of the
input graph and additional vertices corresponding to the classes.
    In Section 6, we investigate the computational impact of the proposed single-class and
multiclass inequalities in solving instances generated following Wang et al. [29] for the
Maximum-Weight Connected Subgraph problem, as well as on computationally more chal-
lenging instances. The computational results indicate that the proposed inequalities lead
to shorter running times and smaller gaps than the inequalities described in the literature.
    Concluding remarks and possible directions for further research are discussed in Sec-
tion 7.


2     Single-class inequalities
In this section, we consider valid (non-trivial) inequalities for P(G, k) where all non-zero
coefficients are associated with a same class c ∈ [k]. Let us denote by (π, π0 ) ∈ Rℓ × R the
inequality πx ≤ π0 where x ∈ Rℓ . An inequality (π, π0 ) ∈ Rnk × R is said to be a single class
inequality for P(G, k) if (π, π0 ) is a valid inequality for P(G, k), and there exists c ∈ [k]
such that πv,b = 0 for all v ∈ V (G) and b ∈ [k] \ {c}. We say that an inequality of P(G, k)
is nontrivial if it is different from (0, 0) and from the nonnegativity constraints (3).

2.1   Facets from P(G, 1) to P(G, k)

single-class inequality of P(G, k) that is also facet defining. In particular, we shall see that
the connectivity inequalities (2) and the indegree inequalities (4) induce facets of P(G, k)
under the same hypothesis shown by Wang et al. [29] for P(G, 1).

  We next use Lemma 1 to characterize inequalities (2) and (4) that are facet-defining for
P(G, k).

Corollary 1. Let k ≥ 2 be an integer. The following hold.

  (i) Inequality (2) is facet defining for P(G, k) if and only if the separator is minimal.

 (ii) Inequality (4) is facet defining for P(G, k) if and only if

      (a) for every u, v ∈ V (G), there exists at most one directed u, v-walk in the corre-
          sponding orientation of G; and
      (b) the orientation of G contains at least two vertices of indegree zero.

Proof. Wang et al. [29] proved that (2), for non-adjacent vertices u, v ∈ V (G), and (u, v)-
separator Z, defines a facet of P(G, 1) if and only if Z is minimal. As G is connected, this
inequality is different from xv ≤ 1 for all v ∈ V (G). Hence (i) holds using Lemma 1.
    To prove (ii), suppose first that (4) induces a facet F of P(G, k), and let D denote the
orientation of G that defines such inequality. By Lemma 1, the corresponding facet F ′ of
P(G, 1) is different from {x ∈ P(G, 1) : xv = 1} for all v ∈ V (G). Wang et al. [29] proved
that F ′ is a facet of P(G, 1) if and only if (a) holds. Due to (a) and F ′ ̸= {x ∈ P(G, 1) :
xv = 1} for all v ∈ V (G), D contains at least two vertices of indegree zero.
    To show the converse, consider an orientation D of G satisfying (a) and (b). Let F ′
be the face of P(G, 1) induced by the inequality (4) which is associated with D. Since D
has at least two vertices with indegree zero, the inequality inducing F ′ contains at least
two coefficients equal to one, and so it is different from xv ≤ 1 for all v ∈ V (G). Since (a)
holds, F ′ is a facet of P(G, 1) by Wang et al. Therefore, the claimed result follows from
Lemma 1.

    The facial structure of the connected k-subpartition polytope on trees was studied before
in the context of the Convex Recoloring problem to model applications in biology
involving phylogenetic trees (see Campêlo et al. [7], and Chopra et al. [12]). In what
follows, we use Lemma 1 to characterize the facets of P(G, k) where G is a tree.
    Korte et al. [20] (Theorem 3.6, p. 168) showed an explicit description of P(G, 1) in terms
of the indegree inequalities when G is a tree.

Theorem 2 (Korte et al. [20] (see [29])). Let G be a tree. It holds that

               P(G, 1) = {x ∈ Rn≥ : x satisfies all indegree inequalities (4)}.

Moreover, each inequality (4) induces a facet of P(G, 1).

Theorem 3. Let G be a tree, let k ≥ 2 be an integer, and let (π, π0 ) ∈ Rnk × R be a
nontrivial single-class inequality of P(G, k). Then, (π, π0 ) induces a facet of P(G, k) if and
only if (π, π0 ) is equal to (4) defined by an orientation of G with at least two vertices of
indegree zero.

Proof. Let F be the facet of P(G, k) induced by (π, π0 ). Applying Lemma 1 on F yields a
facet F ′ of P(G, 1) that is different from {x ∈ Rn : xv = 1} for all v ∈ V (G). By Theorem 2,
F ′ is induced by an indegree inequality (4), and so (π, π0 ) is equal to (4) for some c ∈ [k].
Additionally, the orientation of G defining such inequality contains at least two vertices of
degree zero since G has no cycle, and F ′ ̸= {x ∈ Rn : xv = 1} for all v ∈ V (G).
     Consider an orientation of G having at least two vertices of indegree zero. This orien-
tation defines an inequality (4) of P(G, 1) containing at least two coefficients equal to one,
and so it is different from xv ≤ 1 for all v ∈ V (G). Moreover, such inequality induces a
facet of P(G, 1) by Theorem 2. The result follows from Lemma 1.

    We remark that all (nontrivial) facets of P(G, k) shown by Chopra et al. [12], and by
Campêlo et al. [7] (when G is a tree) are induced by single-class inequalities. As a conse-
quence of the previous theorem, we conclude that all such facets are induced by indegree
inequalities (4).
    For general graphs G, Campêlo et al. [7] devised single-class inequalities for P(G, k)
generalizing (2), and showed sufficient conditions for facetness. These inequalities are a
particular case of the generalized connectivity inequalities introduced next.

4     Multiclass inequalities
We next derive strong valid inequalities for P(G, k) that combine different classes. In
contrast to the single-class inequalities, the multiclass inequalities may have non-zero co-
efficients associated with any subset of classes C ⊆ [k]. As we shall see, the proposed

sets in G instead of pairs of non-adjacent vertices (i.e., stable sets of size two) in G. The
inequalities in Section 4.2 are induced by a collection of pairs of non-adjacent vertices.

4.2    Pairing inequalities
We next introduce inequalities that also generalize (2) to multiple classes. Differently
from (9), each class in these inequalities is associated with a pair of non-adjacent ver-
tices, and assigning one pair of vertices to a certain class may prevent other vertices from
being assigned to another class. Before presenting a formal definition, let us briefly give
some intuition using the following simple example.
     Let k ∈ Z with k ≥ 2, let a, b ∈ [k] with a ̸= b, and let P = v1 . . . v5 be a path.
First note that xv1 ,a + xv2 ,b + xv4 ,b + xv5 ,a − xv3 ,a − xv3 ,b ≤ 2 is a valid inequality for
P(P, k) since it is simply the sum of inequality (2) for vertices {v1 , v5 } in class a, {v2 , v4 }
in class b, and {v3 } as a separator of both pairs. Moreover, it is tight as one could assign
v1 to class a, v2 to class b, and leave the remaining vertices unassigned. The inequality
xv1 ,a + xv2 ,b + xv4 ,b + xv5 ,a − xv3 ,b ≤ 2 dominates the previous one, and it is also valid for
P(P, k). The validity of the last inequality follows from the observation that v2 and v4
cannot be assigned to class b if v1 and v5 are assigned to class a.

is the sum of (10) given by (G, C ′ , {uc , vc }c∈C ′ , Z, γ) for each C ′ ⊆ C such that C ′ is the
set of vertices of a connected component of the class-blocking graph B.


5    Separation of multiclass inequalities
We first show that the separation problem associated with inequalities (9) (if S is given)
is NP-hard by observing that it consists in solving the Minimum Multiway Cut problem
(undirected node version) which is defined as follows.

                                                 23
    Given a graph G, a set of terminals S ⊆ V (G) inducing a stable set in G, and weights
w : V (G) \ S → R≥ , the problem consists in finding a minimum-weight set of non-terminal
vertices Z ⊆ V (G) \ S such that there is no path in G − Z between any pair of distinct
vertices in S, that is, each component of G − Z contains at most one vertex in S. Note that
when |S| = 2, this is exactly the Minimum Vertex Cut problem, and so it is solvable in
polynomial time using a maximum flow algorithm. For |S| ≥ 3, Garg et al. [16] proved that
Minimum Multiway Cut is NP-hard.
    Let us fix k = 1. Given a graph G, a stable set S ⊆ V (G) of G with |S| ≥ 3, and a
vector x̄ ∈ Rn≥ , the separation problem of inequalities (9) on input (G, S, x̄) is equivalent to
finding a minimum-weight set of vertices Z ∗ ⊆ V (G) \ S such that each vertex of S belongs
to a different component of G − Z ∗ , where the weight of each vertex v ∈ V (G) \ S is x̄v . In
other words, solving the separation of (9) is not easier than solving Minimum Multiway
Cut. Therefore the separation problem of inequalities (9), when S is given, is NP-hard
even for k = 1.

6     Computational experiments
In this section we investigate the use of the proposed single-class and multiclass inequalities
for solving random graph instances generated in the same way as the ones described by
Wang et al. [29] for the Maximum-Weight Connected Subgraph problem, as well as for
computationally more challenging instances. In our experiments, we address the following
natural generalization of this classic problem.

Maximum Weight Subgraph Problem.
Input: A graph G, a vertex-weight function w : V (G) → Z (possibly with negative weights),
and k ∈ Z≥ .
Output: A subgraph H of G with P at most k connected components.
Objective: Maximize w(H) := v∈V (H) w(v).

    Wang et al. [29] reported computational experiments on instances consisting of graphs
with 50 vertices that are generated using the Erdős–Rényi random graph model with prob-
ability p ∈ {0.01, 0.02, . . . , 0.25}, and integer weights chosen uniformly at random in the
interval [−50, 50]. In our experiments, we consider a set of larger instances consisting of
random graphs with 100 vertices with probability p ∈ {0.01, 0.02, . . . , 0.10}, integer weights
chosen uniformly at random in the interval [−50, 50], and k ∈ {5, 10, 15, 20, 25}. We remark
that every generated instance with p > 0.10 is solved by all algorithms within less than
0.1 second, and thus we do not report computational results for such instances. For each
combination of p and k, we generate five instances. In this way there are 250 instances in
total of this type.
    Inspired by the complete bipartite example presented in [29], we have also generated
a second instance set containing dense instances that are significantly more challenging
than the previous ones. This set is formed by bipartite graphs with 100 vertices (and both
parts of same size) with edges appearing with probability p ∈ {0.05, 0.10, . . . , 0.50} and
k ∈ {5, 10, 15, 20, 25}. The weights of vertices in one part of the bipartition are chosen
uniformly at random in the interval [−50, 0] and the vertices in the other part with weights
in [0, 50]. Thus, we have 250 bipartite instances in total.

7    Further research
The ILP formulation of P(G, k) given by (1), (2), and x ∈ {0, 1}nk is natural and it can
be adapted to model additional specific constraints of many problems involving connected
partitions. Additionally, one may extend it to model connected k-subpartitions where a
class c ∈ [k] has to be κc -connected (with κc ∈ Z≥ ) by simply imposing

Similar inequalities were used to model a problem in wireless sensor networks which is re-
lated to connected dominating sets (see Ahn and Park [1]). One may also have different
costs/gains when assigning a vertex to a class as in the case of the Convex Recolor-
ing problem. In general, one may easily ensure different properties to the classes in the
(sub)partition using variables indexed by the vertices and classes.
    If k is large and the classes in the subpartition have identical properties, the studied
formulation suffers from symmetry in the sense that any permutation of the classes of a
solution lead to an equivalent solution. To avoid such symmetries, one could alternatively
model P(G, k) using binary variables x̃uv ∈ {0, 1} for every u, v ∈ V (G) to identify whether
u and v belong to the same class in a connected k-subpartition. A future research direction
