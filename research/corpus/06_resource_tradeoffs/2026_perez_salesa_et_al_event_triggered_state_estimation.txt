1. Introduction

    Distributed sensing and estimation have attracted significant attention in recent years, finding applications in areas such as
robot formation control, environmental monitoring and large-scale industrial processes. Thus, many works have been devoted
to developing distributed implementations of popular state estimators, with Kalman filters being a prominent example [1]. In
distributed filtering, several estimator nodes collectively observe the state of a plant, computing state estimates by using a local
sensor measurements and information received from neighboring nodes. Relevant advances have been achieved, such as obtaining
distributed formulations that converge to the solution of the centralized counterpart, e.g., [2–4], and developing event-triggered
filters to reduce communication among estimator nodes [5–7].
    However, several issues remain open. One important limitation is the assumption of synchronicity in the distributed filter. In
most discrete-time formulations, it is implicitly assumed that sampling and filtering at all nodes follow the same synchronized
discretization period. Even when event-triggered communication is considered (see [1,8] and the references therein), the underlying
updates are typically still based on a common global clock. Such an assumption may be unrealistic in practical applications. Most
works that consider asynchronous measurement sampling resort to a ‘‘data synchronization’’ strategy to still apply a synchronous
filter [9–11]. An exception can be found in [12], which is limited to systems without noise, and requires the asynchronous period
to be upper-bounded.
    In addition, event-triggered communication is often applied only to the estimator-to-estimator channel, while continuous
availability of local measurements is still assumed. For cases where sensors and estimator nodes are not co-located, this assumption

may be impractical, and event-triggered communication could also reduce the number of transmissions in this channel. However,
few works have considered this setup. In [13–15], the event-triggered condition is checked periodically, matching the step of a
synchronous filter. Moreover, some restrictive assumptions are made on the noise and observability of the system. Finally, 𝐻∞ filters
are designed in [16,17] under event-triggered measurement transmissions. These works assume that sensors exchange information
with each other, having enough capabilities to aggregate the measurements from neighbors and transmit them to the estimator
node, and even run a local filter (in [17]), while the estimator nodes do not communicate with each other.
    To the best of our knowledge, there is no prior work addressing distributed Kalman filtering under event-triggered measurement
sampling. Note that extending the existing centralized event-triggered Kalman filters to the distributed case is not trivial. Many
works consider triggering conditions that require the sensor to run a local filter [18–20], which assumes computational capabilities
and knowledge of the dynamical model of the system at the sensor side. On the other hand, simple triggering conditions such as the
send-on-delta [21] may cause a divergence of the estimation uncertainty if only the model-based prediction is used by the estimator
during inter-event intervals, as exemplified in [22]. To overcome this issue, some event-triggered Kalman filters exploit the implicit
information in not triggering an event [22–26], but most of them still adopt synchronous filter updates.
    Motivated by this discussion, this work aims to address the current limitations in distributed Kalman filtering with the following
contributions: (1) We develop a distributed filter with fully asynchronous transmissions, where measurement sampling and estimator-
to-estimator communication do not need to be synchronized, by adopting a continuous-time filter formulation. (2) We consider
that each sensor transmits information to an estimator node according to a simple event-triggering condition. The estimator
leverages these measurements and the implicit information between events to provide stable estimates, even in the absence of new
measurement data. (3) We show that event-triggered communication can also be adopted in the estimator-to-estimator channel in
our setup while maintaining the stability of the distributed estimator. (4) We prove the stability of the filter in mean squared error,
and we show that the performance of our distributed filter with reduced communication can approximate that of the centralized
optimal filter with full information by tuning the event thresholds and consensus gain.

3. Event-triggered measurement sampling

   We start by introducing the event-triggering mechanism used by the sensor nodes to decide when to transmit information, as
well as the implicit information that can be extracted by the estimator when the triggering condition is not met.


3.1. Event-triggering mechanism

    The 𝑖th sensor decides its transmission instants via an event-triggering mechanism of the form
          𝑖
         𝜏𝑘+1 = inf{𝑡 > 𝜏𝑘𝑖 | ‖𝐲𝑖 (𝑡) − 𝐲𝑖 (𝜏𝑘𝑖 )‖ ≥ 𝜀𝑖 (𝑡)},                                                                                 (4)

where 𝜀𝑖 (𝑡) > 0 is the event threshold, possibly containing auxiliary dynamics. In order for the estimator to successfully infer implicit
information from the triggering condition (4) and produce stable estimates, we consider the following requirements. First, the
estimator node receiving the measurements from the 𝑖th sensor must have knowledge of 𝜀𝑖 (𝑡), ∀𝑡 ≥ 0. Second, the event threshold
fulfills 𝜀𝑖 (𝑡) ≤ 𝜀max , ∀𝑡 ≥ 0, for some 𝜀max > 0.
    Note that these requirements can be easily met when the event threshold is set to a constant value. In addition, the dynamic
event-triggering mechanism from [22], where the dynamic threshold depends on the evolution of the transmitted measurements,
also enables the extraction of implicit information despite the time-varying threshold, and fulfills the boundedness condition with
probability 1, despite the event threshold being affected by the unbounded Gaussian measurement noise [22, Remark 1]. For such
triggering mechanism, the stability guarantees in this work would hold with probability 1, instead of holding strictly for any noise
realization.
    For simplicity, we now make the following assumption:

Assumption 3. There is no Zeno behavior under the event-triggering mechanisms, i.e., a strictly positive time interval exists between
two consecutive events.

    We will first establish some theoretical results regarding the filter performance under this assumption, isolating its convergence
and stability properties. Then, in Section 7, we will formally prove that Assumption 3 indeed holds with probability 1 despite the
stochastic noise present in the system, and we will detail an easily implementable time regularization strategy to enforce a minimum
inter-event time, discussing its theoretical and practical impact.

7. Strategies for Zeno-freeness

    So far, we have provided our theoretical results while assuming the absence of Zeno behavior under the event-triggering
mechanisms (4)–(22). In this section, we formally prove that Assumption 3 holds with probability 1 for our event-triggered
distributed filters, under some mild considerations. In addition, since only a probabilistic guarantee can be given under the stochastic
noise present in the system, we also discuss an alternative time regularization strategy that can be incorporated to the event-
triggering mechanism to strictly enforce a minimum inter-event time. Finally, we comment on its effect on the overall performance
of the setup.

7.1. Probabilistic avoidance of Zeno behavior

    This section formally establishes conditions ensuring the absence of Zeno behavior. Since the mechanism in (4) is directly
influenced by stochastic measurement noise, whereas the mechanism in (22) acts on filtered estimates that incorporate neighbor
information, each triggering rule is analyzed separately.

7.2. Time regularization


    A simple way to enforce a strictly positive minimum inter-event time is to introduce time regularization, namely, to disable the
triggering condition for a fixed interval after each event. This approach is frequently adopted in stochastic settings, where rare noise
realizations may otherwise induce arbitrarily fast successive events [33]. The triggering rules become

where 𝜏 > 0 and 𝓁 > 0 are regularization parameters. By construction,

    From the perspective of exploiting implicit measurement information between events, disabling the triggering condition during
the interval (𝜏𝑘 , 𝜏𝑘 + 𝜏) implies that such implicit information is not available over that short period. However, the purpose of

exploiting implicit information is to prevent the growth of estimation uncertainty during long inter-event intervals without new
measurements. Since 𝜏 can be chosen arbitrarily small, neglecting implicit information over (𝜏𝑘 , 𝜏𝑘 + 𝜏) does not affect the intended

behavior. Therefore, it is reasonable to treat implicit information as effectively available between events.
    In digital implementations, continuous-time dynamics are approximated with a sampling step ℎ > 0, and the triggering condition
is evaluated once per step. Choosing 𝜏 = ℎ and 𝓁 = ℎ is therefore natural, since it does not impose additional constraints beyond
                                               ̄                ̄
those already induced by sampling. Under this choice, two situations may arise if the triggering condition is violated during the
regularization interval:

     • The violation disappears before the interval ends. No event is generated. This corresponds to a transient excursion of the signal
       that does not persist over one sampling step, and the loss of information is no more significant than from the sampling itself.
     • The violation persists after the interval ends. An event is generated immediately at the next admissible checking instant. In
       the extreme case of persistent violation, events occur periodically with period ℎ, which coincides with full communication at
       every sampling step.

Remark 7. The use of positive event thresholds and time regularization parameters to guarantee well posedness prevents exact
convergence to the centralized filter, as established in item 2 of Theorems 1–2. Indeed, simultaneously setting the event thresholds
and the time regularization parameters to zero may lead to Zeno behavior. Nevertheless, these parameters can be chosen arbitrarily
small, which avoids Zeno behavior while yielding a performance close to that of the centralized filter. The limit case 𝜀max → 0,
𝛿max → 0 is mainly of theoretical interest, as it characterizes convergence to the centralized solution. In practice, selecting zero
thresholds is undesirable since it removes any communication savings.

9. Conclusions

    We have presented a distributed filter for stochastic systems under event-triggered measurement sampling and communication
between estimator nodes. To improve the estimates between sampling events and reduce uncertainty for large inter-event intervals,
the estimators leverage implicit information in not receiving a new measurement, incorporating it as a virtual measurement. Through
formal analysis and simulation experiments, we verify that our solution achieves stable estimates in the mean squared error sense and
that undesirable Zeno behavior is avoided. Moreover, we have shown that, by tuning the consensus gain and the event thresholds,
the performance of the distributed filter can be made arbitrarily close to that of the optimal centralized filter with full information
of the network measurements, while significantly reducing the communication demands of the distributed setup.
